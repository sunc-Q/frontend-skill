# Interval Cartography

*A visual philosophy — Plate series for an imaginary discipline.*

---

**I. The premise**

There exists a phenomenon that cannot be seen directly: an attention that opens its eyes at perfectly even gaps, looks at one thing, records one object, and closes again. What survives is not the phenomenon but the *interval* — the same distance between one appearance and the next. This philosophy begins there. Space is not a background to be filled; it is a measuring instrument. Every composition is a plate from a survey of something that leaves no trace, and therefore must be drawn as the shadow of its own clock. Vast quiet fields are not emptiness, they are the calibrated distance between two observations, held with painstaking exactness so that a single mark inside them can be trusted.

**II. Form and the discipline of the tick**

Form reduces to two primitives: the ring, which is a cycle made honest, and the tick, which is one act of attention made visible. Ticks are drawn in long, patient rows and arcs — hundreds of them, geometrically perfect, evenly weighted — because meaning in this movement is never declared, it is *accumulated*. A field of identical marks read from a distance becomes a texture; read from near, it becomes a record. Nothing is ornament. Nothing repeats by accident. The accumulation must look as though it consumed countless hours of the hand that laid it down, and only a practitioner at the absolute summit of a vanishing craft could place that many identical elements without one of them drifting.

**III. Colour as measurement, not mood**

The palette is paper and ink first: a warm, slightly oxidised ground that behaves like a plate rather than a screen, and a near-black that behaves like a burin. Into this, exactly two interruptions are permitted — one warm signal colour for the element that carries the finding, one cool colour for the element that carries the method. A colour is spent only when a fact is being stated. Saturation is rationed so severely that the moment vermilion appears, the eye has no choice. The calibration between adjacent tones, invisible when correct and ruinous when wrong, is the difference between a chart and a poster, and it must be laboured over in silence.

**IV. Scale, rhythm, and the hierarchy of looking**

Composition is organised as concentric hierarchy: one monumental centre that can be read from across a room, one middle register of patient repetition that rewards three seconds of attention, and one whisper register of small clinical letters that rewards thirty. The registers never compete — each is separated by deliberate air, and the air is dimensioned, not guessed. Type is set thin, wide-tracked, and treated as instrumentation: it labels, it dates, it signs. It never explains. Where a sentence would begin, a ring or a tick belongs instead, because explanation is the failure of the plate.

**V. The signature of the hand**

The finished object should feel archaeological and clinical at once — a document from a discipline that studied ephemera with the reverence usually reserved for stars. Straight where geometry demands, tender where paper shows through ink. No element touches another; no element escapes the margin; no mark exists that could be removed without collapsing the measurement. The work must carry the specific, unmistakable weight of something meticulously crafted, over long hours, by a maker who would rather destroy the plate than ship a single imprecise degree.
